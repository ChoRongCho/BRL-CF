\subsubsection{Baseline Comparison}~\label{exp:sys_baseline}
\noindent\textbf{Oracle}. The oracle comparison includes three baselines with the query timing and selection strategies summarized in Table~\ref{tab:query_strategy_matrix}. KnowNo~\citep{ren2023robots} requests an action choice if its conformal prediction set does not specify a single ordinary action. IntroPlan~\citep{liang2024introspective} extends KnowNo and incorporates retrieved examples and explanations into candidate evaluation before it requests an action choice. POMCP-Q follows the standard POMDP approach to information gathering~\citep{kaelbling1998planning}. It includes Boolean state queries in the POMCP action space and selects them according to expected cumulative reward. Table~\ref{tab:baseline_results} and Fig.~\ref{fig:baseline_results} compare the four methods.

\noindent\textbf{Human}\label{exp:human_eval}. 
We evaluate whether the oracle and VLM trends persist with human feedback using the same robot platforms and task domains. We recruited five participants (one man and four women), with a mean age of $26.4$ years (SD = $5.68$, range $18$--$33$). Each participant completes two trials per method and domain, yielding $10$ trials per condition. Participants receive task instructions and answer queries through the tablet interface. Domain and method orders vary across participants. Appendix~\ref{appx:user} provides the instructions and trial schedules. Fig.~\ref{fig:human_results} reports the results.


Ours achieves $90.00\%$ success in Sort and $100.00\%$ in Harvest, compared with $20.00\%$ and $30.00\%$ for KnowNo, respectively, and requires less planning time in both domains. Across successful and failed trials, total interaction time divided by total query count gives $5.26$ seconds per query for Ours and $3.86$ for KnowNo in Sort, and $2.81$ and $6.42$ seconds in Harvest.


The higher Sort value for Ours is influenced by one P3 trial with $159.44$ seconds of interaction over nine queries. The experimenter observed hesitation after consecutive task failures. Excluding all eight P3 trials across both methods and domains gives $3.07$ seconds per query for Ours and $3.24$ for KnowNo in Sort, and $2.64$ and $4.99$ seconds in Harvest. This additional analysis shows that the relative interaction times in Sort are sensitive to prolonged responses from one participant.


The failure analysis identifies both incorrect human responses and autonomous planner decisions. In Sort, the single failure of Ours follows an incorrect confirmation that an object is visible. KnowNo has four human response failures involving placement before picking and four planner failures involving picking an undetected object without a query. In Harvest, Ours has no failures. Six KnowNo failures follow human selections that skip required preceding actions, and one follows incorrect perception of a rotten tomato without a state-verification query. These cases show that an action-selection query requires participants to interpret execution prerequisites, while autonomous decisions can leave incorrect perception uncorrected.

The timing comparison evaluates Fully-Random, T1--T3, and Ours. In Sort, Fully-Random and T1 achieve $73.50\%$ success, and T2 achieves $74.00\%$. T3 achieves $99.00\%$ compared with $100.00\%$ for Ours, but requires $17.65$ queries compared with $7.83$. Fully-Random, T1, and T2 use $10.25$, $6.65$, and $7.09$ queries respectively. In Harvest, Fully-Random and T1 achieve $73.50\%$ success, while T2 and T3 achieve $63.00\%$ and $68.50\%$ compared with $98.00\%$ for Ours. Mean query counts are $9.04$ for Fully-Random, $7.43$ for T1, $4.05$ for T2, $12.16$ for T3, and $9.16$ for Ours.

These results show that task success depends on the query trigger as well as query selection. T1 replaces random selection in Fully-Random with entropy gain and reduces query count without improving success. Random timing can miss necessary verification because the trigger does not depend on current state uncertainty. T2 can also end queries after the action prediction set narrows to one candidate, even if the state facts required to execute the candidate action remain uncertain. T3 uses expected task reward to determine whether to initiate queries, then selects queries by entropy gain and repeats verification until confidence reaches $\tau$. In Sort, object category verification directly informs the correct bin choice and task reward, so repeated verification may support the high success rate of $99.00\%$. However T3 requires $125.43\%$ more queries than Ours. In Harvest, T3 uses $32.71\%$ more queries than Ours, but the success rate of T3 is $29.50$ percentage points lower. Therefore reward-based timing can request more queries overall and still fail to verify necessary state information at the appropriate time. Thus Ours uses current state confidence as the query trigger and achieves the highest success in both domains with fewer queries than reward-based timing.

With confidence-based query timing fixed, different query selection strategies maintain high success rates but require different numbers of queries. The query selection comparison evaluates Fully-Random, S1, S2, and Ours. Fully-Random achieves $73.50\%$ success in both domains with $10.25$ queries in Sort and $9.04$ in Harvest. S1 retains random selection but uses confidence timing, and success increases to $100.00\%$ and $97.50\%$ respectively. With confidence timing fixed, S1, S2, and Ours all achieve $100.00\%$ success in Sort. Mean query counts are $15.21$ for S1, $22.79$ for S2, and $7.83$ for Ours. In Harvest, S1 and S2 achieve $97.50\%$ success compared with $98.00\%$ for Ours. Mean query counts are $12.68$ for S1, $15.25$ for S2, and $9.16$ for Ours.

Confidence timing maintains $100.00\%$ success in Sort and at least $97.50\%$ in Harvest across all three query selection strategies. Under confidence timing, query selection primarily affects the number of queries needed to reach the required confidence. Random selection does not prioritize facts by their expected reduction in state uncertainty. Reward-based selection evaluates expected task reward without directly measuring the information provided by a query response. Entropy gain selects facts according to the expected reduction in state uncertainty, so the selection criterion matches the confidence requirement that ends a query episode. Relative to S1 and S2, Ours reduces query count by $48.54\%$ and $65.64\%$ in Sort and by $27.72\%$ and $39.92\%$ in Harvest. Thus our query selection method reduces the number of queries required under confidence timing without reducing task success.

These results support the main contribution of our method through the distinct roles of query timing and query selection. Query timing determines whether the robot requests additional information before it executes actions under state uncertainty. Confidence timing achieves high success with random, reward-based, and entropy gain selection, while entropy gain under random timing reduces query count without improving success over Fully-Random. Once confidence timing establishes a high success level, entropy gain reduces the number of queries needed to reach the required confidence. Therefore our method combines a timing criterion that supports task success with a selection criterion that reduces query count at that success level.

\subsubsection{Discussion and Limitation}\label{exp:discussion}
In both Sort and Harvest, our method achieves higher task success rates than all three baselines with oracle responses in simulation. It also achieves higher success rates than KnowNo with VLM and human responses on physical robots. The ablation study clarifies the contributions of query timing and query selection. Confidence-based timing maintains high success rates across different selection strategies, while selection by expected entropy reduction reduces query counts when timing is fixed. The results demonstrate that query timing primarily affects task success, while query selection primarily affects the number of queries required.

In our method, the confidence threshold balances the risk of proceeding under state uncertainty against the burden of additional queries. Insufficient verification can allow incorrect state estimates to affect subsequent actions, but further queries to achieve higher confidence are not always necessary for successful execution. Reducing state uncertainty does not necessarily provide information that improves task performance. The stopping criterion should therefore require sufficient information for reliable execution rather than complete resolution of uncertainty. From this perspective, the confidence threshold determines how much external verification to seek and when to proceed with the remaining uncertainty.

A limitation of our method is that the belief update does not account for errors in external feedback. State hypotheses inconsistent with external responses are removed regardless of response reliability. Incorrect feedback can therefore exclude the true state from the belief and leave the robot confident in an incorrect state. The failures involving incorrect VLM and human responses illustrate the practical impact of feedback errors. Raising the confidence threshold cannot ensure a correct state estimate, because confidence is computed over the remaining hypotheses. Incorporating response reliability into the belief update could preserve plausible alternatives by adjusting hypothesis weights instead of removing inconsistent hypotheses outright.
