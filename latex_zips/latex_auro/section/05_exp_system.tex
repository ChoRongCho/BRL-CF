\subsubsection{Baseline Comparison}~\label{exp:sys_baseline}
\noindent\textbf{Oracle}. The oracle comparison includes three baselines with the query timing and selection strategies summarized in Table~\ref{tab:query_strategy_matrix}. KnowNo~\cite{ren2023robots} requests an action choice if its conformal prediction set does not specify a single ordinary action. IntroPlan~\cite{liang2024introspective} extends KnowNo and incorporates retrieved examples and explanations into candidate evaluation before it requests an action choice. POMCP-Q follows the standard POMDP approach to information gathering~\cite{kaelbling1998planning}. It includes Boolean state queries in the POMCP action space and selects them according to expected cumulative reward. Table~\ref{tab:baseline_results} and Fig.~\ref{fig:baseline_results} compare the four methods.

Fewer queries therefore do not necessarily reduce execution costs.

We distinguish VLM response errors from planner failures. All three failures of Ours in each domain involve incorrect VLM feedback. In WasteSorting, KnowNo has three VLM response failures and $17$ planner failures, with the latter involving autonomous action selections despite unmet preconditions. In TomatoHarvesting, KnowNo has $18$ VLM response failures and $12$ planner failures. Six VLM failures involve repeated detection choices that exhaust the step limit despite feasible alternatives, and the remaining $12$ involve incorrect action selections.

These failures illustrate the importance of both query timing and query content. A single predicted action can still require state verification before execution. An action-selection query requires the VLM to assess robot location, object states, and action preconditions, and its response may still lead to an infeasible or unproductive action. Ours asks the VLM to verify current state facts and uses the updated belief for subsequent planning, leaving action selection to the planner.

\noindent\textbf{Human}\label{exp:human_eval}. 
We evaluate whether the oracle and VLM trends persist with human feedback using the same robot platforms and task domains. We recruited five participants (one man and four women), with a mean age of $26.4$ years (SD = $5.68$, range $18$--$33$). Each participant completes two trials per method and domain, yielding $10$ trials per condition. Participants receive task instructions and answer queries through the tablet interface. Domain and method orders vary across participants. Appendix~\ref{appx:user} provides the instructions and trial schedules. Query timing primarily affects task success. With entropy gain selection fixed, Ours achieves $100.00\%$ success in WasteSorting and $98.00\%$ in TomatoHarvesting, compared with $73.50\%$ in both domains for T1. T2 and T3 also achieve lower success than Ours, although T3 reaches $99.00\%$ in WasteSorting. These comparisons indicate that selecting informative queries alone does not ensure task completion. The timing criterion must also identify when additional state verification is needed.

Query selection primarily affects query count under confidence timing. S1, S2, and Ours all achieve $100.00\%$ success in WasteSorting and at least $97.50\%$ in TomatoHarvesting. Ours requires $7.83$ queries in WasteSorting, compared with $15.21$ for S1 and $22.79$ for S2, and $9.16$ in TomatoHarvesting, compared with $12.68$ and $15.25$, respectively. Thus, entropy gain reduces the queries needed to meet the confidence requirement while maintaining similarly high success.

Specifically, T3 achieves $99.00\%$ success in WasteSorting with $17.65$ queries, compared with $100.00\%$ and $7.83$ for Ours. Once triggered, T3 uses entropy gain selection and continues verification until confidence reaches $\tau$, as in Ours. This shared verification process can support high success after a query episode begins. However, T3 initiates verification only when the highest estimated query value exceeds the highest physical-action value. It can therefore proceed with a physical action even while state confidence remains low. In TomatoHarvesting, T3 achieves $68.50\%$ success with $12.16$ queries compared with $98.00\%$ and $9.16$ for Ours, indicating that more queries overall do not compensate for missed verification. S1 improves success over Fully-Random from $73.50\%$ in both domains to $100.00\%$ and $97.50\%$, respectively, by changing the timing criterion while retaining random selection. Confidence timing can therefore sustain high success even without prioritizing informative facts. Random selection still requires more responses to reach the confidence threshold, and entropy gain in Ours reduces query count relative to S1 by $48.54\%$ in WasteSorting and $27.72\%$ in TomatoHarvesting.

Confidence timing maintains $100.00\%$ success in WasteSorting and at least $97.50\%$ in TomatoHarvesting across all three query selection strategies. Under confidence timing, query selection primarily affects the number of queries needed to reach the required confidence. Random selection does not prioritize facts by their expected reduction in state uncertainty. Reward-based selection evaluates expected task reward without directly measuring the information provided by a query response. Entropy gain selects facts according to the expected reduction in state uncertainty, so the selection criterion matches the confidence requirement that ends a query episode. Relative to S1 and S2, Ours reduces query count by $48.54\%$ and $65.64\%$ in WasteSorting and by $27.72\%$ and $39.92\%$ in TomatoHarvesting. Thus our query selection method reduces the number of queries required under confidence timing without reducing task success.

These results support the main contribution of our method through the distinct roles of query timing and query selection. Query timing determines whether the robot requests additional information before it executes actions under state uncertainty. Confidence timing achieves high success with random, reward-based, and entropy gain selection, while entropy gain under random timing reduces query count without improving success over Fully-Random. Once confidence timing establishes a high success level, entropy gain reduces the number of queries needed to reach the required confidence. Therefore our method combines a timing criterion that supports task success with a selection criterion that reduces query count at that success level.

\begin{comment}

P3은 Ours를 선호하면서도 이러한 반복을 번거롭게 느꼈다. P5는 행동 선택 자체에 특별한 어려움을 느끼지 않았고, 개별 사실을 반복해서 확인해 주는 것보다 명령을 직접 선택하거나 지시하는 방식이 더 편리하다는 이유로 KnowNo를 선호했다. 이는 사용자가 선호하는 참여 방식에도 차이가 있음을 보여준다. 대부분은 행동 결정을 로봇에 맡기는 방식을 편리하게 느꼈고, P5는 로봇의 행동을 직접 지시하는 방식을 선호했다.

실패 분석과 인터뷰를 종합하면, 효과적인 질의를 위해서는 응답자가 제공할 정보와 로봇이 결정할 행동을 명확히 구분할 필요가 있다. Ours는 불확실한 상태에 관한 사실을 참여자에게 확인하고, 그 응답을 이후 계획에 반영한다. 이러한 역할 분담은 행동 선택지의 실행 범위와 선행 조건을 사용자와 로봇이 동일하게 해석해야 하는 문제를 피한다. 인터뷰는 앞서 관찰한 행동 선택 실패를 설명하는 근거를 제공하며, 다수의 참여자가 상태 확인 방식을 더 편리하게 받아들인 이유를 보여준다.

We interviewed all five participants after they experienced Ours and KnowNo in WasteSorting and TomatoHarvesting. Our method asks questions about the current state, while KnowNo asks questions about the next action to execute. We examined how the two methods differed in the judgments needed to answer queries and the perceived effort across the task.

The interviews suggest that action descriptions left room for different interpretations of their effects and preconditions. P1 expected some KnowNo action choices to include the necessary preceding actions, while P4 explained that skipping a required stage led to failure. P2 was unsure whether all offered actions were executable in the current state. These accounts help explain why participants selected actions that did not satisfy the execution requirements discussed in the failure analysis. Ours avoided this ambiguity by asking participants to verify state facts and leaving the interpretation of action preconditions and effects to the planner. Participants could therefore answer without determining which preceding actions were required or whether those actions were included in a proposed choice.


Four of the five participants (P1--P4) described Ours as more convenient and preferred it for the evaluated tasks. They attributed this convenience to checking the current state while the robot determined subsequent actions. These accounts support the proposed division between state verification by the responder and action planning by the robot. Participants also described fatigue from repeatedly answering yes/no questions. P3 found this repetition bothersome even while preferring Ours. P5 reported no particular difficulty in selecting actions and preferred KnowNo because directly selecting or specifying commands felt more convenient than repeatedly confirming individual facts. These responses indicate that preferences also depend on the form of participation. Most participants valued leaving action decisions to the robot, while P5 preferred directing its actions.

Taken together, the failure analysis and interviews suggest that effective queries require a clear division between the information provided by the responder and the action decisions made by the robot. Our method asks participants to verify uncertain state facts and uses their responses in subsequent planning. This division avoids relying on a shared interpretation of the execution scope and preconditions of an action choice. Therefore, the interviews help explain the observed action selection failures and show why most participants found state verification more convenient.

\subsubsection{Discussion}\label{exp:discussion}

Results with oracle, VLM, and human responses support the effectiveness of our method. Query timing and query selection serve complementary roles. The confidence threshold determines when to query based on state uncertainty, while expected entropy reduction determines which information to request. The ablation experiments highlight the importance of both decisions. Effective query selection can reduce the number of queries, but reliable execution also depends on requesting information when state is uncertain.

The objective is to obtain sufficient state information for successful execution while limiting the number of queries. A stricter confidence requirement can increase the number of queries without a corresponding improvement in task success. Thus, reducing uncertainty beyond what is needed for successful execution may introduce unnecessary interaction. These findings suggest that effective querying requires balancing the information needed for reliable execution against the cost of obtaining it.

Beyond task performance, the interviews also provide indirect insight into \textit{how} to query people. Participants described our state questions as easier to judge and reported less need to think before answering than with KnowNo. This feedback supports the use of the proposed state questions for human assistance. However, \textit{how} also includes interaction modalities and UI design, which can affect usability and cognitive workload. We did not compare voice interfaces or alternative UI designs, and their effects on human interaction remain future work.
